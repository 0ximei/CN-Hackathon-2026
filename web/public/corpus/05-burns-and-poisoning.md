# Burns and Poisoning
source: Adapted from public-domain emergency care guidance

## Thermal burns
Cool the burn under cool running water for a full 20 minutes. This remains effective up to three hours after the injury, so start cooling even if time has passed.
Twenty minutes is the target because shorter cooling does not remove enough heat from the deeper tissue, where the burn continues to extend after the flame is out.
Do not use ice or iced water. Extreme cold causes further tissue damage and can drop the body temperature dangerously, especially in children.
Remove clothing and jewellery from the burned area while cooling, but leave anything that is stuck to the skin in place.
Cover the burn with cling film laid in flat sheets, not wrapped around a limb, or with a clean non-fluffy cloth. Wrapping tightly restricts circulation as swelling develops.
Never apply butter, oil, toothpaste, or ointment to a fresh burn. These trap heat and must be painfully scrubbed off later.
Do not burst blisters. Intact blisters are a sterile biological dressing.
Keep the rest of the person warm while cooling the burn, because large burns lose body heat rapidly.

## Assessing burn severity
Superficial burns are red and painful with no blisters, and heal in about a week.
Partial thickness burns are blistered, wet, and intensely painful.
Full thickness burns look white, brown, or charred and leathery, and may be painless because the nerve endings are destroyed. Painlessness is a sign of a worse burn, not a milder one.
Estimate the burned area using the person's own palm including fingers as roughly one percent of their body surface.
Seek urgent care for any burn larger than the person's palm, any full thickness burn, any burn to the face, hands, feet, genitals, or across a joint, and any burn that encircles a limb or the chest.

## Chemical and electrical burns
For a chemical burn, brush off dry powder first, then flush with large volumes of running water for at least 20 minutes, and longer for alkalis, which keep penetrating.
Flush away from the unaffected body so the runoff does not spread the chemical. Protect yourself — do not become a second casualty.
Do not attempt to neutralise an acid with an alkali or the reverse. The reaction generates heat and worsens the injury.
For an electrical burn, do not touch the person until the power is switched off at the source. High-voltage current can arc, so stay at least 18 metres away from downed power lines until the utility confirms isolation.
Electrical injury causes deep tissue damage between an entry and exit wound that is far worse than the skin suggests, and can disturb heart rhythm for hours. All electrical injuries need medical assessment.

## Smoke inhalation
Suspect airway burns if there is soot around the mouth or nose, singed nasal hair, a hoarse voice, coughing black sputum, or breathing difficulty after a fire in an enclosed space.
Airway swelling can develop over several hours and then close the airway rapidly. Anyone with these signs needs hospital assessment even if they feel well initially.
Move to fresh air and give oxygen if available. Carbon monoxide poisoning can be present with normal-looking skin, so do not rely on colour.

## Poisoning and overdose
Do not induce vomiting. It causes further damage on the way back up, particularly with corrosives and petroleum products, and risks fluid entering the lungs.
Identify what was taken, how much, and when. Keep the container, packaging, or plant sample for the medical team.
If the substance is corrosive and the person is fully alert, small sips of water or milk can dilute it. Give nothing by mouth to a drowsy person.
For skin contact, remove contaminated clothing and wash the skin with copious water for at least 15 minutes.
For eye contact, irrigate the open eye with clean water for at least 20 minutes, sweeping from the inner corner outward.
If the person becomes unresponsive but is breathing, place them in the recovery position, because vomiting is common in poisoning.
