# Environmental Emergencies
source: Adapted from public-domain wilderness medicine guidance

## Hypothermia
Hypothermia begins when core temperature falls below 35 degrees Celsius. It kills in wet, windy conditions well above freezing, so do not dismiss it in mild weather.
Mild hypothermia shows as uncontrollable shivering, clumsiness, and slurred speech. The person often insists they are fine — impaired judgement is itself a symptom.
Severe hypothermia shows as shivering that has stopped, rigid muscles, confusion, and a slow weak pulse. Paradoxical undressing, where the person removes clothing despite the cold, is a late and ominous sign.
Move the person out of wind and rain. Remove wet clothing by cutting it off rather than making them move, and replace it with dry insulation including a hat.
Insulate from the ground, which steals more heat than the air does. Put a sleeping mat, pack, or spare clothing underneath them.
Give warm sweet drinks only if they are fully alert and can swallow. Never give alcohol, which dilates skin vessels and accelerates heat loss.
Handle a severely hypothermic person gently and keep them horizontal. Rough handling can trigger a fatal heart rhythm.
Do not rub the limbs or place them in hot water. Rewarming the limbs first drives cold blood back to the core and can drop the core temperature further.

## Frostbite
Frostbite starts as numb, waxy, white or grey-yellow skin that feels hard or rubbery. Superficial frostbite still has soft tissue underneath; deep frostbite feels wooden throughout.
Do not thaw a frozen limb if there is any chance it will refreeze. A refreeze cycle causes far worse tissue destruction than staying frozen during evacuation.
Rewarm by immersion in water at 37 to 39 degrees Celsius — warm to the touch but not hot — for 20 to 30 minutes, until the tissue is red and pliable.
Rewarming is intensely painful and pain relief will be needed. Do not rub the tissue, and do not use dry heat such as a fire or stove, because numb skin burns without the person feeling it.
Separate affected fingers and toes with dry gauze, and do not walk on thawed feet.

## Heat exhaustion and heat stroke
Heat exhaustion presents with heavy sweating, pale clammy skin, headache, nausea, cramps, and a fast weak pulse. The person is still mentally clear.
Move them to shade, lie them down with legs raised, loosen clothing, and give cool water with a pinch of salt or an oral rehydration solution. Recovery should begin within 30 minutes.
Heat stroke is different and is a true emergency. The key sign is altered mental state: confusion, aggression, seizures, or unconsciousness, with a core temperature above 40 degrees Celsius. The skin may be hot and dry, or still sweating.
Cool aggressively and immediately. Whole-body immersion in cold water is the most effective method. Otherwise soak the person, fan them continuously, and place ice packs at the neck, armpits, and groin.
Cooling in the first 30 minutes determines survival. Do not delay cooling in order to arrange transport — cool first, move second.
Stop active cooling once the person becomes coherent, to avoid overshooting into hypothermia.

## Dehydration and oral rehydration
Early dehydration shows as thirst, dark urine, and reduced urine output. Later signs are dizziness on standing, a fast pulse, sunken eyes, and skin that stays tented when pinched.
An oral rehydration solution can be mixed from six level teaspoons of sugar and half a level teaspoon of salt in one litre of clean water. Getting the proportions right matters: too much sugar worsens diarrhoea.
Give small sips frequently rather than large volumes at once, which usually causes vomiting. Aim for a cup after each loose stool.
A person who cannot keep fluids down, or who has stopped passing urine for more than eight hours, needs intravenous fluids.

## Snake bite
Keep the person still and calm. Movement and a rising heart rate speed venom through the lymphatic system.
Immobilise the bitten limb with a splint and keep it at or below heart level. Remove rings, watches, and tight clothing before swelling begins.
Mark the leading edge of the swelling with a pen and write the time, repeating every 15 minutes. This record helps clinicians judge envenomation.
Do not cut the wound, do not attempt to suck out venom, do not apply ice, and do not use a tourniquet. All of these cause harm without removing venom.
Photograph the snake only if it can be done from a safe distance. Do not attempt to catch or kill it — a second bite is a common and avoidable disaster.
Antivenom is the only definitive treatment. Evacuate to a facility that stocks it.
