# Disaster Response and Survival
source: Adapted from public-domain civil defence and survival guidance

## Making water safe to drink
Boiling is the most reliable method. Bring water to a rolling boil for one minute, or three minutes above 2000 metres altitude where the boiling point is lower.
Filter cloudy water through cloth first and let it settle, because suspended particles shield microorganisms from both heat and chemical disinfection.
Chemical disinfection with unscented household bleach uses two drops of 5 percent sodium hypochlorite per litre of clear water, doubled for cloudy water. Mix and wait 30 minutes; the water should smell faintly of chlorine.
Chlorine and iodine do not reliably kill cryptosporidium. Where that is a risk, filter or boil instead.
Solar disinfection works in clear plastic bottles laid on a reflective surface in full sun for six hours, or two days if overcast.
Do not drink seawater, urine, or alcohol when short of water. All three consume more body water than they provide.

## Finding and collecting water
Look in valley bottoms, at the base of cliffs, and where vegetation is greenest. Follow animal tracks downhill; they usually converge on water.
Collect dew before dawn by dragging absorbent cloth through grass and wringing it out.
A transpiration bag — a clear plastic bag tied around a leafy non-poisonous branch in sunlight — yields a small amount of clean water over a day.
Rainwater collected directly in a clean container needs no treatment.
Ration sweat, not water. Rest in shade during the heat of the day and travel at night; drinking your supply while working in the sun outperforms conserving it while dehydrating.

## Shelter and staying warm
Shelter is the priority ahead of water and food in cold or wet conditions, because exposure kills in hours rather than days.
Choose a site out of the wind, off the valley floor where cold air pools, away from dead trees and rockfall, and above any possible flood line.
Insulate underneath before building anything overhead. Dry leaves, grass, or a pack beneath the body prevents the ground conducting away body heat.
Keep the shelter small. A space just large enough for your body is warmed by your body; a large shelter is not.
Keep clothing dry and avoid sweating. Wet insulation loses most of its value, so remove layers before working hard and replace them immediately afterward.

## Signalling for rescue
Three of anything — three fires, three whistle blasts, three flashes — is the recognised distress signal.
A signal mirror is visible for many kilometres. Aim by holding two fingers at arm's length toward the target and flashing the reflected light across them.
A whistle carries far further than the voice and costs almost no energy. Use it in preference to shouting.
Make ground signals large: at least 3 metres per stroke, in straight lines with high contrast against the background. V means assistance required, X means medical assistance required.
Stay put once you are reported missing unless you must move for safety. A stationary group is far easier to find than a moving one.

## Earthquake
During shaking, drop to hands and knees, cover your head and neck under a sturdy table, and hold on. Do not run outside during the shaking, when falling glass and masonry cause most injuries.
If you are in bed, stay there and cover your head with a pillow.
If you are outdoors, move to an open area away from buildings, trees, and power lines, and stay there.
After the shaking stops, expect aftershocks. Check for injuries, then check for gas leaks, and shut off the gas only if you smell or hear escaping gas.
Do not use matches or light switches until you are certain there is no gas leak.
If you are trapped, cover your mouth, tap on a pipe or wall in a rhythmic pattern, and use a whistle if you have one. Shout only as a last resort, because it exhausts you and causes you to inhale dust.

## Flood
Move to high ground immediately and do not wait for instructions if water is rising.
Do not walk through moving water. Fifteen centimetres of moving water can knock an adult off their feet, and the surface hides washed-out ground.
Do not drive through floodwater. Sixty centimetres of water floats most vehicles, and most flood deaths involve vehicles.
Assume all floodwater is contaminated with sewage, fuel, and debris. Wash thoroughly after any contact and cover wounds.
Electricity and standing water are lethal together. Do not enter a flooded room where the power may still be on.
After a flood, discard any food that has touched floodwater, and do not drink tap water until authorities declare it safe.

## Fire safety and evacuation
Get out and stay out. Do not return to a burning building for possessions.
Crawl low under smoke, where the air is cooler and clearer. Most fire deaths are from smoke inhalation, not burns.
Before opening a door, feel it with the back of your hand. If it is hot, use another route.
If your clothes catch fire, stop, drop, and roll, covering your face with your hands.
Close doors behind you as you leave. A closed door significantly slows a fire's spread and buys survivable time.
Agree a meeting point outside so it is immediately clear whether anyone is unaccounted for.
