# Trauma and Sudden Collapse
source: Adapted from public-domain emergency care guidance

## Fractures and splinting
Suspect a fracture where there is deformity, grating, inability to bear weight, or pain that is sharply localised over a bone.
Splint the limb in the position you found it. Do not attempt to straighten an angulated fracture unless the limb below is pale, cold, and pulseless — in which case apply gentle traction until circulation returns.
A splint must immobilise the joint above and the joint below the fracture, otherwise the bone ends still move.
Pad the splint well and check circulation, sensation, and movement beyond the splint before and after applying it. If fingers or toes go pale, blue, or numb, the splint is too tight.
An open fracture, where bone has broken the skin, needs the wound covered with a sterile dressing and urgent antibiotics. Do not push protruding bone back inside.

## Spinal injury
Suspect a spinal injury after any fall from height, a diving accident, a vehicle collision, or any injury with head trauma and altered consciousness.
Symptoms include neck or back pain, tingling, weakness or numbness in the limbs, and loss of bladder control.
Keep the head, neck, and spine in a straight line. Hold the head still with both hands and tell the person not to move.
Do not move the person unless they are in immediate danger, such as fire or rising water. If you must move them, use a log roll with several people keeping the spine aligned as a single unit.
If the person is unconscious and breathing but you must leave them, place them in the recovery position while keeping the spine as aligned as possible. A protected airway takes priority over a perfectly immobilised spine.

## Shock
Shock is the failure of circulation to deliver oxygen to tissue. It is caused by blood loss, severe burns, dehydration, severe infection, or anaphylaxis.
Signs are pale, cold, clammy skin, a fast weak pulse, rapid shallow breathing, thirst, restlessness or anxiety, and later confusion and unconsciousness.
A falling blood pressure is a late sign. Do not wait for it — treat on the basis of the skin, pulse, and mental state.
Lay the person flat and raise the legs about 30 cm unless there is a leg fracture, spinal injury, or breathing difficulty.
Keep them warm with insulation above and below, loosen tight clothing, and give nothing by mouth.
Treat the cause: stop the bleeding, cool the burn, give adrenaline for anaphylaxis. Shock cannot be treated by positioning alone.

## Head injury and concussion
Any blow to the head followed by confusion, memory loss, headache, dizziness, nausea, or visual disturbance is a concussion and needs rest and observation.
Red flags requiring urgent care are: a seizure, repeated vomiting, worsening headache, unequal pupils, clear fluid from the nose or ears, weakness on one side, increasing drowsiness, or any period of unconsciousness.
Deterioration can occur hours after the injury as bleeding accumulates inside the skull. Someone must stay with the person for at least 24 hours and wake them periodically.
Do not give aspirin or ibuprofen after a head injury, because both interfere with clotting. Paracetamol is safer.

## Seizures
Do not restrain the person and do not put anything in their mouth. Swallowing the tongue is a myth; forcing objects into the mouth breaks teeth and blocks the airway.
Clear hard objects away, cushion the head, and note the time the seizure started.
Once the convulsion stops, roll the person into the recovery position and allow them to sleep. Confusion and drowsiness afterwards are normal and can last an hour.
Call for emergency help if the seizure lasts more than five minutes, if a second seizure follows without full recovery, if it is the person's first seizure, if they are injured, pregnant, or diabetic, or if the seizure occurred in water.

## Anaphylaxis
Anaphylaxis is a rapid, whole-body allergic reaction. Suspect it when a swollen face or tongue, a widespread rash, wheeze, throat tightness, or collapse follows a sting, food, or medication.
Give adrenaline by auto-injector into the outer thigh immediately, through clothing if necessary. Adrenaline is the only treatment that reverses anaphylaxis; antihistamines are far too slow.
Lay the person flat with legs raised. Do not stand them up or let them walk, because sudden upright posture during anaphylaxis can cause fatal cardiac arrest.
A second dose may be given after five minutes if there is no improvement.
Everyone who receives adrenaline must be observed in hospital, because symptoms can return several hours later.
