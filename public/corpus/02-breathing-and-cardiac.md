# Breathing and Cardiac Emergencies
source: Adapted from public-domain resuscitation council guidance

## Adult CPR
Check for danger, then check for a response by shouting and squeezing the shoulders. If there is no response, shout for help and open the airway by tilting the head back and lifting the chin.
Look, listen, and feel for normal breathing for no more than 10 seconds. Occasional gasping is not normal breathing and is a sign of cardiac arrest.
If breathing is absent or abnormal, begin chest compressions. Place the heel of one hand in the centre of the chest, the other hand on top, and interlock the fingers.
Compress hard and fast: 5 to 6 cm deep, at 100 to 120 compressions per minute. Allow the chest to recoil fully between compressions.
Give 30 compressions followed by 2 rescue breaths. If you are unwilling or unable to give breaths, continuous compressions alone are far better than nothing.
Swap rescuers every two minutes if possible, because compression quality falls sharply with fatigue.
Continue until the person shows signs of life, a defibrillator is ready to use, or you are physically unable to continue.

## Child and infant CPR
For a child, use one or two hands and compress to about one third of the chest depth. For an infant, use two fingers in the centre of the chest just below the nipple line.
Give 5 initial rescue breaths before starting compressions, because cardiac arrest in children is usually caused by a breathing problem rather than a heart problem.
Use a compression to breath ratio of 30 to 2 if you are alone, or 15 to 2 with two trained rescuers.
Do not tilt an infant's head as far back as an adult's. Over-extending the neck closes a small airway rather than opening it.

## Choking
If the person can cough forcefully, encourage them to keep coughing. Do not intervene while an effective cough continues.
If the cough becomes silent or weak, or they cannot breathe or speak, give 5 sharp back blows between the shoulder blades with the heel of your hand, leaning them forward.
If back blows fail, give 5 abdominal thrusts: stand behind, place a fist above the navel, grasp it with the other hand, and pull sharply inward and upward.
Alternate 5 back blows and 5 abdominal thrusts until the obstruction clears or the person becomes unresponsive.
If they become unresponsive, lower them to the ground and begin CPR, checking the mouth for the dislodged object before each set of breaths.
For an infant, use 5 back blows and 5 chest thrusts. Never use abdominal thrusts on an infant.
Anyone who has received abdominal thrusts should be assessed medically for internal injury.

## Suspected heart attack
Typical symptoms are central crushing chest pain or pressure lasting more than a few minutes, often spreading to the jaw, neck, or left arm, with sweating, nausea, and shortness of breath.
Women, older adults, and people with diabetes more often present without classic chest pain — instead with sudden fatigue, breathlessness, or indigestion-like discomfort.
Sit the person down, leaning back with knees bent. This position reduces the workload on the heart.
If they are not allergic and not already bleeding, give one 300 mg aspirin and ask them to chew it slowly. Chewing gets it into the bloodstream faster than swallowing.
Do not let them walk to transport. Exertion during a heart attack can trigger cardiac arrest.

## Stroke — the FAST check
Face: ask them to smile, and look for one side drooping.
Arms: ask them to raise both arms, and see whether one drifts down.
Speech: ask them to repeat a simple sentence, and listen for slurring or the wrong words.
Time: if any of these are abnormal, note the time symptoms began and seek emergency care immediately.
The time of onset determines which treatments are possible, so record it precisely. If they woke with symptoms, the relevant time is when they were last seen well.
Give nothing to eat or drink. A stroke frequently impairs swallowing, and fluid entering the lungs causes pneumonia.
