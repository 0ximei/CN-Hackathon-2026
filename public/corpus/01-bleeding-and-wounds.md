# Bleeding and Wounds
source: Adapted from public-domain first aid guidance (WHO/ICRC field manuals)

## Severe external bleeding
Severe bleeding is the leading preventable cause of death after injury. Act within seconds.
Apply firm, direct pressure to the wound with the heel of your hand, using any clean cloth as padding. Press hard enough that it is uncomfortable — light pressure does not stop arterial bleeding.
Do not remove soaked dressings. Add more padding on top and keep pressing, because lifting the dressing tears away the clot that is forming.
If the limb is bleeding uncontrollably and direct pressure fails, apply a tourniquet 5 to 7 cm above the wound, never over a joint. Tighten until the bleeding stops and the pulse below disappears. Write the time of application on the casualty's forehead or the tourniquet itself.
Once a tourniquet is applied, do not loosen or remove it. Loosening releases accumulated toxins and restarts the bleeding.
Lay the casualty down and keep them warm. Blood loss causes rapid heat loss, and cold blood clots poorly.

## Embedded objects in a wound
Do not pull out an embedded knife, glass shard, or stake. The object may be sealing a severed vessel, and removing it can cause sudden fatal bleeding.
Build up padding on either side of the object until the padding is higher than the object, then bandage across the padding without pressing on the object itself.
Apply pressure around the object, not on it.

## Cleaning and dressing a minor wound
Wash your hands first if water is available. Irrigate the wound with clean drinking water under pressure — a plastic bottle with a pinhole in the cap works well. Aim for at least 250 ml for a small wound.
Do not use hydrogen peroxide or undiluted antiseptic inside an open wound. Both damage healing tissue and slow recovery.
Remove visible grit with clean tweezers. Pat dry, apply a thin layer of antibiotic ointment if available, and cover with a sterile non-adherent dressing.
Change the dressing daily, or sooner if it becomes wet or dirty.

## Recognising wound infection
Infection usually appears 24 to 72 hours after injury. Watch for spreading redness, increasing rather than decreasing pain, swelling, heat, pus, or a foul smell.
Red streaks running from the wound toward the body, fever, or chills indicate the infection is spreading through the lymphatic system. This is an emergency requiring antibiotics.
Mark the edge of the redness with a pen and note the time. If the redness advances past the mark within a few hours, the infection is progressing rapidly.
Any wound from an animal bite, a puncture from a dirty object, or a wound contaminated with soil carries tetanus risk and needs medical assessment.

## Amputation and severed parts
Control the bleeding at the stump first. The casualty's life takes priority over the severed part.
Wrap the severed part in clean, slightly damp gauze, seal it in a plastic bag, and place that bag in a second bag containing cold water and ice.
Never place the severed part directly on ice or in water. Direct contact with ice causes freezing damage that makes reattachment impossible.
Label the bag with the time of injury and keep it with the casualty.
